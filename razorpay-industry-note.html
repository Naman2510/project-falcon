01

Executive Summary

India's UPI rail processed 228.3 billion transactions worth ₹299.7 lakh crore (~$3.4 trillion) in calendar 2025, up 29% in volume and 20% in value — the largest real-time payments network in the world by transaction count, and still growing faster than any comparable market. But the shape of that growth is changing: NPCI-linked commentary puts FY2025-26 volume growth at roughly 25%, down from 42% the year before, as the base matures and the easiest merchant and consumer conversion is largely done.

Against that backdrop, the economics of the industry are being rewritten from Delhi, not from the market. On 4 August 2026, Parliament amended the Payment and Settlement Systems Act to permit — for the first time since UPI went to zero merchant-discount-rate (MDR) in 2020 — a modest fee on large-merchant UPI transactions. That single legislative change is the most consequential development for payment aggregator economics in six years, because it opens a revenue pool on volumes that today generate nothing for the payment service providers (PSPs) that route them.

At the same time, three of India's largest private payments platforms — Pine Labs (listed November 2025), PayU (targeting a 2025-26 listing), and Razorpay (confidential DRHP filed 2026) — are converging on public markets within roughly the same 18-month window, at valuations running 20-45% below their 2021-22 private peaks. This note lays out the structure, regulation, competitive set, and global read-across behind that moment, as context for the companion Razorpay valuation and pitch materials.

Market Structure & Sizing

UPI's scale is now a macro fact as much as a fintech one. NPCI-reported data shows volumes climbing from 172.2 billion transactions in 2024 to 228.3 billion in 2025, with December 2025 alone setting a fresh monthly record of 21.63 billion transactions worth ₹27.97 lakh crore. Daily transaction counts are projected to cross 1 billion per day by FY2026-27.

Market-sizing estimates for India's broader payments industry vary considerably by scope — a reminder that "market size" numbers should always be read against their stated definition rather than compared across sources. On the broadest definition (all electronic payments, consumer and commercial), one widely cited estimate puts the market at $421.5 billion in 2025, growing to $960.2 billion by 2030 — a 17.9% CAGR. A narrower "digital payment platform" definition from a different provider shows a much smaller $6.75 billion base, growing faster (22.3% CAGR) off that lower starting point. Both are directionally consistent — high growth off an already-large and rapidly compounding base — but the tenfold difference in absolute size is a useful caution against quoting a single TAM figure without its scope attached.

03

Value Chain

Four layers sit between a UPI or card transaction and a merchant's bank account, and Razorpay's own M&A history (see companion Comps memo, §04) traces almost exactly onto them:

  • Rails / networks — NPCI (UPI, RuPay), Visa, Mastercard. Public-utility economics; PSPs plug into these, they don't compete with them.
  • Payment aggregators / gateways — Razorpay, PayU, Cashfree, CCAvenue, PhonePe Pay Gateway. This is the layer that owns the merchant relationship, holds the RBI Payment Aggregator license, and is the layer this note is about.
  • Issuers & acquiring banks — the regulated balance-sheet layer (HDFC, ICICI, Axis, SBI, and others) that PAs route through and increasingly disintermediate for smaller-ticket UPI flows.
  • Adjacent embedded layers — banking-as-a-service (Razorpay's "RazorpayX"), payroll (Opfin), BNPL/lending distribution, and loyalty/rewards (PoshVine, POP) — the layer every major PA is now bolting on to diversify beyond pure processing take-rate, which UPI's zero-MDR regime made structurally thin.

That last point is the single most important structural fact in this note: because UPI carried no merchant fee at all until the August 2026 amendment, every India-focused PSP has spent the last five years building non-processing revenue lines (lending distribution, payroll, loyalty, subscriptions/BaaS) precisely because its core rail didn't monetize. The MDR reversal (§04) partially — not fully — relieves that pressure.

04

Regulatory Landscape

Payment Aggregator licensing

RBI's 2020 Payment Aggregator/Payment Gateway guidelines forced every PA above a net-worth threshold through a formal authorization process; the largest incumbents (Razorpay, PayU, Cashfree) received final RBI authorization over 2023-2025, closing a multi-year period of licensing uncertainty that had constrained new-merchant onboarding for some players. This is now largely resolved industry-wide and is a tailwind, not a live risk, for the incumbents in §05.

Competition law precedent: the BillDesk lesson

Prosus-backed PayU's $4.7 billion agreed acquisition of BillDesk (announced August 2021) received Competition Commission of India clearance in September 2022 — thirteen months after signing — only for the deal to lapse automatically days later when conditions precedent weren't satisfied before the contractual long-stop date. The deal's collapse is the single most important data point for anyone modeling a large domestic payments M&A transaction in India: regulatory clearance is achievable, but the clock runs long enough that closing risk on a scale deal is real, not theoretical (see companion Comps memo, §03, and the process recommendation in the one-pager, §04).

The MDR reversal — the live catalyst

UPI merchant transactions have carried zero MDR since January 2020, with the government partially offsetting PSPs' infrastructure costs through an annual incentive scheme that industry estimates put at covering only 11-14% of actual costs. The Payments Council of India formally petitioned the PMO in March 2025 to reinstate a 0.3% MDR on large-merchant UPI volumes; the Finance Ministry publicly denied any such plan as recently as June 2025. Parliament nonetheless amended the Payment and Settlement Systems Act on 4 August 2026, creating the first legal basis since 2020 for the RBI to authorize a merchant fee on UPI and RuPay debit transactions above a to-be-set turnover threshold — explicitly preserving zero-cost UPI for consumers and small merchants, and pitched as "far lower" than existing card MDRs.

Why this matters for valuation — UPI is roughly two-thirds of India's digital payment value and has generated essentially no take-rate for PSPs since 2020. Even a small, large-merchant-only MDR converts a meaningful slice of currently unmonetized volume into fee-bearing volume for the first time in six years. None of the trading comps in the companion memo reflect this — it is a India-specific, forward-looking catalyst that a public-market investor pricing Razorpay purely off global PSP multiples could easily underweight relative to a strategic acquirer who models it explicitly.

06

Global Read-Across

Three global patterns are directly relevant to how India's payments platforms will likely evolve and be valued:

  • Scale consolidation among mature acquirers. Global Payments' $24.25 billion agreement to acquire Worldpay (April 2025) — alongside divesting its Issuer Solutions business to FIS in the same transaction — shows that even the largest acquirers see further scale, not organic growth, as the primary lever left in mature markets. India's PAs are nowhere near this stage, but it is the trajectory PayU, Razorpay, and PhonePe are all implicitly betting they can reach.
  • Margin expansion through embedded infrastructure, not payments per se. Adyen trades at the richest multiple in the peer set (7.0x revenue, 50% EBITDA margin) precisely because its platform model captures acquiring, risk, and issuing economics in one stack — the same integrated-stack logic behind Razorpay's own BaaS and payroll bolt-ons.
  • Strategic appetite for next-generation rails. Stripe's $1.1 billion acquisition of stablecoin infrastructure startup Bridge (closed February 2025, a >5x step-up on its 2024 Series A) signals that leading global PSPs are willing to pay option-value premiums for programmable-money infrastructure well ahead of proven revenue. India's own CBDC (e-Rupee) push is the domestic analogue worth watching over the next cycle.

07

Capital Markets: the Fintech IPO Wave

India's payments and broader fintech sector is running through an unusually concentrated listing window. Pine Labs listed on 14 November 2025, pricing its $440 million IPO below its original range and still finishing its first trading day up 14%, at a resulting market capitalization of roughly $3.3 billion — a 35-45% discount to its 2022 private peak of over $5 billion, and the second-largest India fintech listing of the year after online brokerage Groww's ~$750 million debut. PayU, having sold its global business and refocused on India, has pushed its own listing target from an originally-eyed 2024 window to late 2025/2026, while raising smaller pre-IPO capital (including a ~$300 million minority stake sale process run through HSBC). Razorpay's confidential DRHP, filed in 2026 after completing its US-to-India domicile reversal in 2025, is the third leg of the same story.

The pattern across all three is consistent: substantial markdowns to 2021-22 zero-rate-era private valuations, converging listing windows that will compete for the same pool of India fintech-sector IPO demand, and — for the two names public so far — a market that, on the day, priced conservatively but rewarded the stock on debut. That combination is exactly what makes the dual-track logic in the companion one-pager worth board-level attention rather than a rubber-stamp "proceed with IPO" decision.

08

Key Risks & Outlook

  • Growth deceleration. UPI volume growth is projected to slow to ~25% in FY2025-26 from 42% the prior year — a maturing base means future PA growth increasingly has to come from monetization (take-rate, cross-sell) rather than raw volume, exactly when public-market investors will be scrutinizing growth durability hardest.
  • MDR implementation risk. The August 2026 legislative change creates legal authority for an RBI-set large-merchant UPI fee, but the threshold, rate, and phase-in timeline are not yet finalized; merchant-lobby pushback delayed the debate for over a year already (the Finance Ministry denied any plan as recently as June 2025) and could shape a smaller or slower-phased fee than the sector hopes for.
  • Regulatory/closing risk on any scale M&A. The BillDesk precedent (§04) means a strategic sale process for any major India PA should be expected to run long and carries real deal-break risk even after antitrust clearance.
  • Crowded capital-markets window. Three-plus major PSPs pursuing India listings within an 18-24 month span increases the risk that any one of them prices below where it would in a less congested window.
  • Global multiple compression. Fiserv's 2025 share-price decline and broadly compressed processor multiples (PayPal at 7.4x EV/EBITDA, well below its historical range) mean the global comp set a public investor will reach for is, at this moment, a conservative one — reinforcing rather than easing the pricing pressure India's own listings are already seeing.

Net: the structural growth story in Indian digital payments remains intact — a sub-30-year-old real-time rail still compounding at high-20s% volume growth off a ~$3.4 trillion annual base — but 2026 is the year the industry's economics (MDR), ownership (the IPO wave), and competitive intensity all inflect at once. That combination of tailwind (MDR) and headwind (multiple compression, growth deceleration) arriving simultaneously is exactly why the Razorpay-specific valuation work in the companion memo triangulates across methods rather than leaning on any single multiple.
